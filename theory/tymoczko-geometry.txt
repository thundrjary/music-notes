TYMOCZKO — TONALITY AS INTERACTING CONSTRAINTS
==============================================

CENTRAL CLAIM
-------------
Tonality is not a list of chord progressions or rules. It is a system
for creating coherence out of several constraints acting at once.


THE FIVE COMPONENTS
-------------------
1. Conjunct melodic motion   — lines move short distances
2. Acoustic consonance       — some sonorities feel stable
3. Harmonic consistency      — successive chords belong to one family
4. Limited macroharmony      — a small pitch reservoir over a span
5. Centricity                — some pitch acts as home

Treat these as knobs, not requirements:

    melodic smoothness
    harmonic consonance
    harmonic similarity
    pitch-collection limitation
    tonal-center strength

Styles differ by knob setting. This is far more general than
"tonal = V-I, atonal = no V-I."


1. VOICE LEADING IS THE DEEPEST ORGANIZING PRINCIPLE
----------------------------------------------------
Efficient voice leading = conjunct motion in all voices at once.
Harmony is the byproduct of simultaneous melodies, not a sequence
of vertical objects. Counterpoint and harmony constrain each other
and cannot be separated.

Instead of reading:   Cmaj -> Abmaj -> Emin -> Fmin
ask:                  what stays? what moves by semitone?
                      what moves by whole step? what must leap?

At the keyboard, see this:

    C  E  G
    |  |  |
    B  F  G

before deciding on a Roman numeral.


2. A CHORD HAS MOTION AFFORDANCES
---------------------------------
Chord structure determines which paths lead out of it.

    clustered (B-C-Db)      -> internal motion, stasis, texture,
                               activity without progression
    even/distributed (CEG)  -> smooth harmonic change, chromatic
                               transformation, progression

So don't only ask "what color is this chord?"
Ask "what can this chord easily become?"


3. SCALE, MACROHARMONY, AND CENTER ARE THREE DIFFERENT THINGS
-------------------------------------------------------------
    Scale        = the ruler. Defines what counts as a step, so the
                   same interval spans different step-counts in
                   different scales.
    Macroharmony = the reservoir. Which pitch classes have actually
                   sounded across this span.
    Centricity   = the gravity. Which pitch feels like home.

Collection and center are independent variables:

    small collection    + strong center
    small collection    + weak center
    chromatic collection + strong center
    chromatic collection + weak center

    scale != key != tonic != pitch collection


4. GENERALIZED KEYS
-------------------
    generalized key = pitch collection + center

Major and minor become two options among many:

    D Dorian centered on D
    D Dorian centered on F
    whole-tone centered on E
    octatonic centered on C#
    melodic minor centered on Bb

Later tonal music gains its vocabulary by freely pairing centers
with diatonic modes, pentatonic, whole-tone, octatonic, and the
minor collections. This is how to hear impressionism without
flattening it into Roman numerals.


5. MODULATION IS VOICE LEADING AT A LARGER SCALE
------------------------------------------------
    chord A  -> move a few voices -> chord B
    collection A -> move one or two pitches -> collection B

    D major:  D E F# G  A B C#
    A major:  A B C# D  E F# G#
    change:   G -> G#

An entire modulation can be a single semitone event. Debussy often
transforms a theme's whole harmonic world by moving one pitch.


6. THE SAME PROCESS RUNS AT EVERY SCALE
---------------------------------------
    note -> note
    chord -> chord
    phrase collection -> phrase collection
    key area -> key area

At each level the ear rewards small displacement, common tones,
continuity, and controlled introduction of new pitches. So ask
what changed in the beat, the measure, the phrase, the section.


7. CHROMATICISM IS NOT LOSS OF STRUCTURE
----------------------------------------
Chromatic harmony is motion through a larger voice-leading space —
more complex than the seven diatonic triads, but still systematic,
not deviation from tonal logic.

When Chopin surprises you, ask first:

    which voices moved, and by how little?
    which pitches held?
    which chord type was preserved?

Only then reach for an exotic label.


8. COHERENCE IS TWO-DIMENSIONAL
-------------------------------
    horizontal — each melodic line moves convincingly
    vertical   — simultaneous pitches form related sonority types

That pairing describes the whole extended common practice:
Renaissance counterpoint, Bach, Chopin, Wagner, Debussy, jazz.
Only the definition of an acceptable vertical sonority changes.


9. HARMONIC IDENTITY SURVIVES CHANGING CHORDS
---------------------------------------------
Harmonic consistency means family resemblance, not repetition:

    triads            -> triadic world
    dominant sevenths -> dominant world
    quartal chords    -> quartal world
    clusters          -> cluster world
    extended tertian  -> jazz/impressionist world

So: pick a sonority vocabulary, then voice-lead through it —
rather than switching chord construction whenever you want color.


10. A SMALL PITCH VOCABULARY CREATES COHERENCE
----------------------------------------------
Tonal music typically holds its macroharmony to five to eight
pitches over moderate spans. Color then comes from reordering,
voicing, register, doubling, voice leading, shifting the center,
and drawing different subsets — not from constant new chromatics.

    choose an interesting 6-8 note universe
    explore it deeply
    then change one note

That one note can feel enormous.


11. THE LISTENER NEED NOT HEAR THE MECHANISM
--------------------------------------------
The theory is composer-centered: it asks what concepts help you
make this music, not what labels describe it afterward.

The listener hears arrival, tension, brightness, distance, stasis.
They do not hear "two voices moved by T1" or "macroharmony changed
by one pitch class."

    Analyze technically; compose perceptually.


WHAT TO MAKE AUTOMATIC
----------------------
 1. Hear each chord as moving voices, not a block.
 2. Spot common tones, semitones, whole steps instantly.
 3. Track smoothness separately in soprano, inner voices, bass.
 4. Identify the current sonority family.
 5. Know the active collection without assuming its tonic.
 6. Hear the center independently of the collection.
 7. Notice when one note replaces the whole collection.
 8. Hear modulation as voice leading between collections.
 9. Hold a harmonic language steady while voices move freely.
10. Understand a chord by what it can become.
11. Think at several scales at once:
    voice -> chord -> phrase -> collection -> tonal region.
12. Read chromatic notes as voice-leading events, not departures.
13. Prefer small changes with large color consequences.
14. Treat the five components as independent controls.
15. Judge by ear, not by elegance of mechanism.


THE DEEPEST COMPRESSION
-----------------------
1. MINIMIZE MOTION.
   Voices want short, intelligible paths.

2. PRESERVE IDENTITY WHILE CHANGING SOMETHING.
   Hold common tones, sonority type, collection, or center steady
   while another variable moves.

3. SEPARATE COLLECTION FROM CENTER.
   Ask independently: what notes exist? which one is home?

4. APPLY THE SAME THINKING AT EVERY SCALE.
   Notes into notes, chords into chords, collections into
   collections, regions into regions.

This is the bridge from counterpoint to late-Romantic chromaticism
to Debussy/Ravel scalar harmony. The later chapters elaborate it:
the nineteenth century expands chromatic voice-leading space, the
twentieth combines those techniques with constrained collections.

CAVEAT: this is a compositional and theoretical framework, not an
established psychological theory. Tymoczko leaves the empirical
psychology to specialists.

TYMOCZKO CH. 2 — HARMONY AND VOICE LEADING
==========================================

WHAT THE CHAPTER IS FOR
-----------------------
Musical set theory tries to catalogue every chord available to a
composer. That turns out to be harder than it looks, because:

    - chords have a real geometry, and distance in it is
      voice-leading size
    - "transposition," "inversion," "triad," "chord type" are all
      relative to a scale; two chords can share a category in one
      scale and not another
    - chord tones differ in importance (rootedness, centricity)
    - the objects being compared can themselves be progressions

So the chapter rebuilds the machinery from the ground up, and ends
at one result: acoustic consonance, efficient voice leading, and
harmonic consistency are three faces of the same fact.


1. LINEAR PITCH SPACE
---------------------
Pitched sound is periodic; frequency = 1/t. Listeners track ratios
between frequencies, not absolute positions — transpose a tune and
every frequency is multiplied by the same c.

Ratios are awkward, so take a logarithm:

    p = 69 + 12 * log2(f/440)

Now distance is subtraction, |p - q|, not division. The unit is the
semitone; middle C = 60; A440 = 69. The space is continuous, so
60.17 is a real address (17 cents above middle C). Letter names are
just shorthand for these numbers.


2. CIRCULAR PITCH-CLASS SPACE
-----------------------------
Octave-related pitches share a quality (chroma). Ignore octave
information and the line curls into a circle: pitch class.

Pitch-class space is not a separate space — it is made from pitch
space by discarding information, so it inherits names, "above,"
"below," and distance. Distance between two pitch classes = the
shortest distance between any two pitches belonging to them.

    Analogy: pitch class : pitch  ::  time of day : time
    "C to C# is one semitone" is "8am to 9am is one hour" —
    one statement summarizing infinitely many.

INTERVALS AS PATHS. Tymoczko's unusual move: an interval is a path,
not just a destination.

    C ---(+4)---> E     four semitones clockwise
    C ---(-8)---> E     eight semitones counterclockwise

Both land on E. They are different intervals. Paths may even wrap
the circle more than once. Traditional theory ("go from C to E
however you like") cannot express why the ascending-4 cases in
different octaves are closely related and the descending-8 case
is not.


3. TRANSPOSITION AND INVERSION
------------------------------
Only two transformations preserve distance:

    transposition  Tx(p) = p + x            translation / rotation
    inversion      Ixy(p) = (x + y) - p     reflection

Transpositions have a size, and size is audible: five octaves is
obvious, 0.00001 semitone is not. Inversions have no size — for any
distance, some pair moves by it — but they reverse direction, so
they change character more dramatically than transposition.

Every pitch inversion has one fixed point, possibly between the
keys; each note moves twice its distance from it. Every pitch-class
inversion fixes two antipodal points on the circle.

Inversionally related chords still sound broadly alike: major and
minor triads differ, but both sit far from a chromatic cluster.


4. MUSICAL OBJECTS AND THE OPTIC SYMMETRIES
-------------------------------------------
Understanding music is discarding information. Formally: define
symmetry operations that leave an object's identity intact; objects
related by them form an equivalence class.

Start with a basic musical object — an ordered series of pitches,
uninterpreted, e.g. (C4, E4, G4). Absent symmetries, it is utterly
unrelated to (E4, C4, G4).

Five operations (OPTIC):

    O  octave        move any one note to another octave
    P  permutation   reorder which voice has which note
    T  transposition move all notes equally
    I  inversion     turn the object upside down
    C  cardinality   duplicate a note into a new voice

Rameau's chord = classify by pitch-class content, ignoring order
and register = the OPC class. Hence {C, E, G}.

    Term                            Symmetry
    ------------------------------  --------
    chord                           OPC
    chord type (transp. set class)  OPTC
    set class                       OPTIC
    multiset of pitch classes       OP
    chord of pitches                PC
    tone row (ordered pc set)       OC

Geometrically: same chord type = one rotates into the other on the
pitch-class circle, so they share the same sequence of adjacent
distances. Major triads read 4-3-5 clockwise from the root; minor
reads 4-3-5 counterclockwise from the fifth. Same set class = same
arc sequence, either direction.

There is no single correct level of abstraction. Different purposes
need different information; all 32 combinations of OPTIC mean
something.


5. VOICE LEADINGS VS. CHORD PROGRESSIONS
----------------------------------------
Now treat progressions — sequences of objects — as objects in their
own right. The wrinkle: each symmetry can now be applied two ways.

    uniform     same operation to both chords
    individual  different versions to each chord

Two categories matter most:

    voice leading      -> uniform P
                          maps notes to notes; keeps voice identity
                          notated with a single arrow  ->

    chord progression  -> individual P and C
                          harmonies only, no mapping of notes
                          notated with a double arrow  =>

    "move C major to C# diminished by raising C a semitone"
        = voice leading
    "move C major to C# diminished however you like"
        = chord progression

A PITCH-CLASS VOICE LEADING drops octaves but keeps the paths:

    (G, G, B, D, F) --(5, 0, 1, -2, -1)--> (C, G, C, C, E)

Same schema in any register. But G moving -7 instead of +5 is a
different voice leading: the path matters. Numbers are omitted when
every voice moves within -6 < x <= 6 (tritones ascend by fiat).

These abstract schemas are what a composer actually stores — "you
turn C major into F major by moving E up a semitone and G up two."
Memorizing every registral instance separately would be impossible.

    Term                              Symmetry
    --------------------------------  -----------
    voice leading in pitch space      uniform P
    voice leading in pc space         uniform OP
    pitch-space chord progression     individual PC
    chord progression                 individual OPC
    path in pitch-class space         uniform O


6. COMPARING VOICE LEADINGS
---------------------------
UNIFORM T-RELATED: same pattern at a new level.

    (C, E, G) -> (C, F, A)
    (G, B, D) -> (G, C, E)

INDIVIDUAL T-RELATED: each chord transposed differently.

    (C, E, G) -> (C, F, A)     [transpose 1st by 7, 2nd by 6]
    (G, B, D) -> (F#, B, D#)

Uniform I-related voice leadings are mirror images: same distances,
reversed directions. Useful practically — the semitonal voice
leadings between major and minor triads come in I-related pairs,
which halves what you have to remember.

WHY INDIVIDUAL RELATEDNESS EARNS ITS KEEP. It names
similarity-with-difference that is otherwise hard to state.

    Tristan opening: half-diminished slides to a dominant seventh
    Brahms Op. 76/4 and Debussy's Faune do the same thing with the
    second chord transposed up a semitone (Debussy also moves the
    whole thing by tritone and revoices).

All three: a two-semitone voice leading between half-diminished and
dominant seventh, root to root, third to third, fifth to fifth,
seventh to seventh. What was borrowed is not the progression but
the schema — theoretically savvy theft.

It also explains sequences that break their own pattern: Mozart's
C minor Fantasy ends a descending sequence on F#7 instead of the
expected F7, and Beethoven Op. 31/3 inserts a second into a chain
of thirds. The voice leadings stay structurally analogous, so the
ear hears continuity where strict transposition has been abandoned.


7. VOICE-LEADING SIZE
---------------------
Polyphony = independent melodies articulating efficient voice
leadings between meaningful harmonies. That requires comparing
sizes. But is (C,E,G)->(Db,F,Ab) bigger or smaller than
(C,E,G)->(C,E,A)? The first moves more total distance; the second
has a larger maximum single move.

Theorists have proposed many precise metrics and practice cannot
adjudicate between them, so Tymoczko stays neutral. A metric is
"reasonable" if:

    1. it depends only on how far individual voices move,
       with more motion meaning a larger voice leading
    2. voice leadings with crossings are no smaller than their
       uncrossed alternatives

The second encodes a division of labor: prefer many voices moving a
little to few voices moving a lot. No composer or pedagogue has
ever proposed a measure violating either.

These two constraints alone already imply real results — e.g. the
more evenly a chord divides the octave, the smaller the voice
leadings to its transpositions; and E and Ab major are the major
triads nearest C major. So most of the book can say "small" and
"large" without picking a metric. Read "efficient voice leading"
as "all voices move short distances."


8. NEAR IDENTITY
----------------
Exact equivalence is too rigid. A pure fifth, an equal-tempered
fifth, and a meantone fifth belong to different chord types under
strict definitions, though musicians treat them as versions of one
object. Likewise {C,E,G,Bb} and {C,Eb,G,Bb} are obviously more
alike than either is to {F#,G,Ab,A}.

Identity is one end of a continuum — and the continuum tracks voice
leading. Tiny voice leading to the just-intonation version;
moderate to the minor seventh; large to the cluster.

So define nearness by voice leading, and extend it:

    two chord types are similar if some transpositions of them are
    linked by a small voice leading

    C diminished is NEARLY TRANSPOSITIONALLY related to F minor
    {C,Db,G} is NEARLY INVERSIONALLY related to {Eb,G,A}

Chapter 3 makes this literal: there are geometrical spaces in which
distance between chords IS minimal voice-leading size.


9. THE CENTRAL RESULT — NEAR SYMMETRY
-------------------------------------
The question: when can harmonic consistency and efficient voice
leading coexist? Chapter 1 answered "when a chord divides the
octave very evenly or very unevenly." The real answer is broader:

    ONLY NEARLY SYMMETRICAL CHORDS CAN DO BOTH.

A chord near a perfectly symmetrical chord inherits that symmetry
as efficient voice leading. The proof is a gluing argument, and it
runs identically for all three symmetries:

    A -> S            small, since A is near S
    F(A) -> F(S) = S  apply F uniformly; S is F-symmetric
    retrograde and glue:  A -> S -> F(A)
    delete the middle:    A -> F(A)   still small

TRANSPOSITIONAL. Symmetric chords either divide the octave evenly
or decompose into equal subsets that do ({B,C,F,F#} = two tritones).

    C major is near the C augmented triad
    (C, E, G) -> (C, E, G#) -> (B, E, G#)
    therefore  (C, E, G) -> (B, E, G#)   C major near E major

Same argument with {C,E,F#,B} near the two-tritone {B,C,F,F#} gives
the efficient tritone link behind jazz tritone substitution.

Limiting case: as A approaches exact T-symmetry, the voice leading
A -> Tx(A) shrinks to zero.

Cost note: when a larger chord exploits a smaller chord's symmetry,
it must pay in extra voices. Hence tonal routines like
(C, C, E, G) -> (A, C, F, F). The major triad is special not only
for near-evenness but for being reasonably close to the tritone.

INVERSIONAL. Symmetric chords arrange their notes around an axis
through two antipodal points.

    (C, D#, E) -> (C, D, E) -> (C, Db, E)
    therefore  (C, D#, E) -> (C, Db, E)

Real case: F half-diminished is a semitone from a diminished
seventh; invert that voice leading and E7 is too. Glue, delete the
diminished chord, and you have the Tristan progression. The famous
gesture exploits the near I-symmetry of those two chord types.

PERMUTATIONAL. "Permutationally symmetrical" just means the chord
has duplicate notes, like {C,C,C}. Nearby chords are the clusters.

    (B, C, Db) -> (C, C, C) -> (C, Db, B)
    therefore  (B, C, Db) -> (C, Db, B)

An efficient voice leading from a chord to ITSELF, reshuffled. That
is why clusters support internal motion without progression.

    T near-symmetry -> efficient VL between transpositions
    I near-symmetry -> efficient VL between inversions
    P near-symmetry -> efficient VL from a chord to itself

THE CONVERSE HOLDS. If A connects efficiently to some transposition
or inversion of itself, A is nearly symmetrical under T, I, or P.
These three are the only symmetries there are. So every instance of
efficient voice leading between structurally similar chords, in any
scale or tuning, reduces to them — which is what lets Part II read
centuries of Western practice as variants of one technique.

CAUTION: the relevant symmetrical chord may not exist on your
keyboard. The pentatonic {0,2,5,7,10} is near the perfectly even
five-note division {0, 2.4, 4.8, 7.2, 9.6}, which is unplayable in
twelve-tone equal temperament — yet it is why the pentatonic links
smoothly to its transpositions. Understanding the discrete universe
requires the continuous space it sits inside.


10. ACOUSTIC CONSONANCE = NEAR EVENNESS
---------------------------------------
Fourier: any periodic function is a sum of sine waves at integer
multiples of a fundamental. In sound these are partials.

Helmholtz: two pitches are consonant when their partials align or
are far enough apart not to interfere; dissonance is roughness from
partials close but misaligned. The basilar membrane does roughly
this decomposition. Broadly accepted, if incomplete.

Western instruments have strong low partials, so partials match at
simple whole-number ratios — 2:1, 3:2, 4:3, 5:4, 6:5. A larger
chord is consonant when it is rich in consonant intervals, and that
requires its notes to be spread fairly evenly around the circle.

The elegant part: the major triad is the first three distinct pitch
classes of the harmonic series, 3:4:5, which divides the octave in
FREQUENCY space exactly evenly. Take the logarithm into pitch space
and exact evenness becomes near evenness — arcs of about 5, 4, 3
semitones. Consonance implies near evenness (not the reverse).


THE PAYOFF
----------
The basic sonorities of Western music are optimal twice over, for
unrelated reasons:

    as objects  -> acoustically consonant, pleasing in themselves
    as nodes    -> nearly even, hence linked to their transpositions
                   by efficient voice leading

Tymoczko's parable: ask God for chords that let you combine
efficient voice leading with harmonic consistency, and you get the
perfect fifth, major triad, dominant seventh. Ask instead for
chords that simply sound good, and you get the same suitcase.

Hence Western counterpoint is a lucky accident. Musicians chose
consonant chords because they sounded restful. Only later did it
emerge that those same chords could carry harmonically consistent
polyphony — and centuries of contrapuntal technique followed from
a coincidence that we can now explain as a consequence of the
hidden symmetries of circular pitch-class space.

COMPOSER'S CHEATSHEET — TYMOCZKO, CH. 1-2
=========================================
Practical extractions. Everything here is something you can do at
the keyboard or on the page.


I. THE FIVE DIALS
-----------------
Every piece is a setting of five independent controls. Set them
deliberately; don't inherit them from habit.

    melodic smoothness      how far individual voices move
    consonance level        how restful the sonorities are
    harmonic consistency    how much chords resemble each other
    macroharmony size       how many pitch classes are in play
    centricity              how strongly one pitch is home

TIP: To generate a style, freeze four dials and sweep one.
TIP: To fix a passage that sounds muddled, find the dial you're
     accidentally moving and lock it.
TIP: "Atonal" usually just means low centricity + low harmonic
     consistency. You can drop either one alone.


II. VOICE-LEADING FIRST
-----------------------
Harmony is the residue of simultaneous melodies. Stop composing
chord-then-chord.

For every change, run this checklist BEFORE naming anything:

    which notes stay?
    which move by semitone?
    which move by whole step?
    which are forced to leap?

TRICK — the three-question test for any surprising chord:
    1. which voices moved, and how little?
    2. which pitches were held?
    3. what chord type survived?
Only after those three, reach for a label.

TRICK — division of labor: many voices moving a little beats few
voices moving a lot. If one voice is jumping a seventh, look for a
rewrite where three voices move by two or three semitones instead.
This is also the rule that makes voice crossings expensive.

TRICK — the path matters, not just the destination. C up 4 to E and
C down 8 to E are different musical events. When a line's direction
is the point, write the path, not the interval.


III. CHOOSE CHORDS BY WHAT THEY CAN BECOME
------------------------------------------
Chord structure determines available exits. Ask affordance, not
just color.

    NEARLY EVEN chords (triads, dom7, pentatonic, diatonic)
        -> smooth motion to their own transpositions
        -> good for progression, modulation, chromatic drift

    CLUSTERED chords (B-C-Db)
        -> smooth motion to reorderings of themselves
        -> good for internal activity WITHOUT progression,
           texture, stasis, shimmer

    IN BETWEEN (whole-tone, oct subsets, {C,D,E})
        -> smooth motion to specific inversions/transpositions
        -> good for controlled, limited-move harmonic worlds

If your harmony feels stuck, you may have chosen a sonority whose
exits you don't actually want.


IV. THE NEAR-SYMMETRY ENGINE (the single most usable trick)
-----------------------------------------------------------
This is a recipe for generating smooth, unusual progressions on
demand. It works every time.

    STEP 1  Pick a perfectly symmetrical chord S.
              augmented triad          {C, E, G#}
              diminished seventh       {C, Eb, F#, A}
              whole-tone collection
              two stacked tritones     {B, C, F, F#}
              a unison/doubling        {C, C, C}
              an inversional axis chord {C, D, E}

    STEP 2  Find chord A a semitone or two away from S.

    STEP 3  Apply your transformation F (a transposition, an
            inversion, or a reordering) to the whole voice leading.

    STEP 4  Glue A -> S -> F(A), then delete S.

    RESULT  A -> F(A), an efficient voice leading you probably
            wouldn't have found by ear.

WORKED EXAMPLES YOU CAN STEAL:

    via augmented triad
        C major -> E major, one voice by semitone, two held
        (this is the whole Romantic third-relation vocabulary)

    via diminished seventh
        F half-dim -> E7    (the Tristan move)

    via two tritones
        {B,C,E,F#} -> its tritone transposition
        (this is the jazz tritone substitution)

    via doubled note
        {B,C,Db} -> itself, reshuffled
        (cluster music that moves without going anywhere)

RULE OF THUMB: any chord that sits within a semitone or two of a
symmetrical chord will have a secret smooth exit. Go hunting for
the symmetrical chords near your favorite sonorities.

COST: when a bigger chord borrows a smaller chord's symmetry, you
must add a voice. Budget for the doubling. Example:
(C, C, E, G) -> (A, C, F, F).

CONVERSE, useful as a diagnostic: if you WANT a smooth link between
a chord and its own transposition or inversion and can't find one,
the chord isn't near any symmetry. Change the chord, not the
voice leading.


V. SEPARATE COLLECTION FROM CENTER
----------------------------------
Three different questions, asked independently:

    SCALE        what counts as a step here?
    MACROHARMONY which pitch classes are actually live right now?
    CENTER       which pitch feels like home?

Any combination is available:

    small collection + strong center      diatonic tonality
    small collection + weak center        modal/impressionist float
    chromatic + strong center             late Wagner, Chopin
    chromatic + weak center               atonality

GENERALIZED KEY = collection + center. Major and minor are two
entries in a large catalogue:

    D dorian on D          D dorian on F
    whole-tone on E        octatonic on C#
    melodic minor on Bb    pentatonic on any of its five notes

TRICK: keep the collection and move the center. Same notes,
completely new light. Cheapest color change available.


VI. WORK IN A CONSTRAINED PITCH UNIVERSE
----------------------------------------
Tonal music typically holds 5-8 pitch classes over a moderate span.
Color comes from working the collection, not escaping it.

    choose an interesting 6-8 note universe
    exhaust its subsets, voicings, registers, doublings, centers
    THEN move one note

That one changed note will feel enormous — precisely because you
earned it. If you add chromatic notes constantly, none of them
lands.


VII. MODULATION IS VOICE LEADING, ZOOMED OUT
--------------------------------------------
    chord A      -> move a few voices     -> chord B
    collection A -> move one or two pitches -> collection B

    D major:  D E F# G  A B C#
    A major:  A B C# D  E F# G#
    difference:  G -> G#

Compose modulations the way you compose chord changes: minimum
displacement, maximum common tone.

DEBUSSY MOVE: don't modulate the theme — move ONE pitch under it
and let the theme's whole harmonic world change around it.

The same discipline applies at every level. Ask, of each span:
    what changed in this beat?
    in this measure?
    in this phrase?
    in this section?
Good answers at every resolution: small displacement, retained
common material, controlled introduction of the new.


VIII. HOLD THE LANGUAGE, MOVE THE VOICES
----------------------------------------
Harmonic consistency is family resemblance, not repetition. Pick a
sonority vocabulary and voice-lead through it:

    triads / seventh chords / dominant-family / quartal /
    clusters / extended tertian

You can travel enormous harmonic distances and still sound
coherent, as long as the vertical TYPE stays recognizable.

ANTI-PATTERN: switching chord construction whenever you want more
color. That reads as loss of nerve, not as development.

The whole extended common practice is one recipe:
    make every horizontal line convincing
    while keeping the vertical sonorities related
Only the definition of an acceptable vertical sonority changes,
from Palestrina to Ligeti.


IX. STEALING WITHOUT GETTING CAUGHT
-----------------------------------
Borrow the SCHEMA, not the progression. Take a voice leading you
love and transpose only the second chord. Structurally analogous,
audibly new.

    Tristan:  half-dim -> dom7, two-semitone motion
    Brahms and Debussy: same schema, second chord up a semitone
    (Debussy also moves the whole thing by tritone and revoices)

Same trick rescues sequences that need to break pattern: keep the
voice leadings structurally analogous and the ear hears continuity
even when exact transposition has been abandoned. Mozart's C minor
Fantasy ends on F#7 where F7 was expected; nobody flinches.


X. WHY THE OLD CHORDS WORK
--------------------------
Consonant sonorities are optimal twice, for unrelated reasons:

    they sound restful in themselves (aligned partials, simple
    ratios: 2:1, 3:2, 4:3, 5:4, 6:5)

    they divide the octave nearly evenly, so they connect to
    their own transpositions efficiently

Practical consequence: if you want smooth transposition-to-
transposition motion in a new harmonic language, choose sonorities
that are NEARLY EVEN, whether or not they're consonant. Evenness,
not consonance, is what buys you the voice leading.

Corollary: the relevant symmetrical chord need not be playable. The
pentatonic works because it's near a perfectly even five-note
division that doesn't exist in twelve-tone equal temperament. Think
in continuous space even when writing for piano.


XI. WORKING DISCIPLINE
----------------------
    ANALYZE TECHNICALLY, COMPOSE PERCEPTUALLY.

The listener hears arrival, tension, brightness, distance, stasis.
They do not hear "common-tone retention 75%." Use the theory to
generate and control possibilities. Then judge by ear. An elegant
mechanism that sounds bad is a bad mechanism.


QUICK REFERENCE — FOUR PRINCIPLES
---------------------------------
    1. MINIMIZE MOTION.
       Voices want short, intelligible paths.

    2. PRESERVE SOMETHING WHILE CHANGING SOMETHING.
       Hold common tones, sonority type, collection, or center
       steady while one other variable moves.

    3. SEPARATE COLLECTION FROM CENTER.
       What notes exist? Which one is home? Two questions.

    4. APPLY THE SAME THINKING AT EVERY SCALE.
       Notes into notes, chords into chords, collections into
       collections, regions into regions.


DRILL LIST — WHAT TO MAKE AUTOMATIC
-----------------------------------
    hear chords as moving voices, not blocks
    spot common tones, semitones, whole steps instantly
    track smoothness separately in soprano, inner voices, bass
    name the current sonority family without thinking
    know the live collection without assuming its tonic
    hear the center independently of the collection
    notice the single note that replaced the whole collection
    hear modulation as voice leading between collections
    identify the symmetrical chord nearest any sonority you use
    understand a chord by what it can become
    read chromatic notes as voice-leading events, not departures
    prefer small changes with large color consequences


CAVEAT
------
This is a compositional and theoretical framework, not a
psychological theory. Tymoczko explicitly leaves the empirical
listening research to specialists. Treat these as generative tools,
not as claims about what audiences perceive.
