## Framing

- Non-harmonic tones are notes the melody plays that aren't in the underlying chord; a more useful name for the purposes of this course is **linear dissonance**.
- The approach is to start from the traditional dissonance formulas and then push them into less traditional territory.

## Preconditions — when this works at all

- **Keep the harmony simple.** With a three-note chord the ear can tell which notes belong and which don't. With very complicated chords it's impossible to say what's in the harmony, and the whole technique collapses.
- **Separate melody from chords in the texture.** Use register, or tone color (chords in strings, melody in trumpet) — some distinction is required one way or another.
- **Let the melody move faster than the chords.** Not strictly necessary, but much easier and what happens in the majority of cases.
- **Why the old formulas exist:** Western music began as vocal music, so it favored what was easy to sing. Singing a note that's in a sounding chord is easy; finding a note outside it is much harder. The traditional formulas are all ways of arriving at a non-chord note easily.
- **Why they still matter for instruments:** from the ear's point of view they make a dissonance sound like it belongs, rather than like a wrong note or an accident.
- **The Bach exception:** in four- or five-part counterpoint, two or three parts can hold the harmony clear while the others are dissonant at the same time. As long as the overall harmony stays clear, that's not a problem — and it opens harmonic possibilities you wouldn't otherwise have.

## Principle 1 — Preparation (how you arrive)

Never attack a dissonance out of nowhere. Four methods, easiest to hardest:

- **Common tone / suspension** (Ex. 1A) — instead of moving the top note when the chord changes, hold it over. Easiest of all, because the singer already has the note and has nothing new to do.
- **Stepwise** (Ex. 1B) — chord tone, dissonant note, chord tone, all a step apart. The in-between note creates a tension that gives the music interest the plain version didn't have.
- **Ornamental** (Ex. 1C) — the same as stepwise, but with a small detour on the way. Key detail: the detour note is itself in the chord, so it creates no need for resolution of its own.
- **By leap** (Ex. 1D) — the first note is in the first chord, then you leap to the dissonance. The most difficult method.

## Principle 2 — Resolution (how you leave)

A dissonance is by definition tense, in conflict with the chord, and you can resolve it with varying degrees of tension.

- **Down by step** (Ex. 1A) — the easiest resolution.
- **Up by step** (Ex. 1E) — the same thing, only the direction differs.
- **Ornamental resolution** (Ex. 1F) — an ornamented version of 1E, with a note stuck in between. This kind of ornamentation can be taken quite far.
- **Resolution in the wrong voice** (Ex. 1G) — a dominant seventh whose dissonance resolves in the bass rather than in the soprano. Good for dramatic effect. **Caveat: use it consistently.** If everything else resolves in the same voice and one instance doesn't, it just sounds like a mistake; used regularly it yields interesting harmonic colors.
- **Resolve to another dissonance** (Ex. 1H) — change the chord at the moment of arrival so the note you land on is also dissonant. Requires an overall more dissonant style to work.
- **Delayed resolution** (Ex. 1I) — the furthest extension of the 1F principle. A dissonant A♭ appears, and several notes later a G emerges that derives from that A♭; the G is dissonant and falls to F, also dissonant, before finally reaching E as the resolution. **This works as long as it stays within the listener's memory** — within that limit the ornamentation can become extremely elaborate and still make perfect sense.
