This is the opening of Persichetti's *Twentieth-Century Harmony*, chapter 1. The principles layer roughly like this:

## The founding axiom

1. **Total freedom of material.** Any tone may follow any tone; any tones may sound together; any group may follow any group; any degree of tension may appear in any medium, dynamic, or duration. Nothing is forbidden a priori.
2. **Freedom is not license — the burden shifts from rule to justification.** Success depends on the contextual and formal conditions that prevail, plus the composer's skill and soul. Persichetti removes prohibition and replaces it with accountability to context.
3. **The interval is the entry point.** Understanding harmony begins with understanding intervals — and melodic and harmonic intervals are treated as one subject, since an interval's quality holds whether its notes sound successively or simultaneously.

## Physical basis and the twelve categories

4. **Constant physics, variable meaning.** An interval's acoustic properties never change; what changes is what it means in a given working context. Both halves matter — the theory is neither purely acoustic nor purely conventional.
5. **Overtone derivation.** Intervals formed low in the overtone series read as consonant; those from the upper partials read as dissonant. Tension has a physical correlate, not just a stylistic one.
6. **Temperament as reduction.** The chromatic tempered scale collapses an unlimited number of ratios into twelve intervals that inherit the character of their overtone-series counterparts.
7. **Consonance and dissonance form a graded continuum, not a binary.** Six textural classes: open consonance (P5, octave), soft consonance (M/m 3rds and 6ths), mild dissonance (M2, m7), sharp dissonance (m2, M7), the perfect fourth (either), the tritone (neutral or restless).
8. **Inversional pairing.** In Ex. 1-1 the brackets connect inversions: 1↔11 (m2/M7, sharp), 2↔10 (M2/m7, mild), the thirds with their sixths (soft), P5 with the octave (open). Inversion generally preserves tension class.
9. **Two exceptions are boxed off deliberately.** The P4 (no. 5) and tritone (no. 6) sit at the center and are set apart — the P4 breaks the inversion rule (its inversion, P5, is an open consonance while the P4 itself is unfixed), and the tritone inverts to itself.
10. **The tritone is the least stable interval** because it bisects the octave; it has no stable position to invert away from.
11. **Size does not track tension.** Reading Ex. 1-1 left to right, the categories interleave rather than intensify — the sharpest dissonance is the smallest interval and also the second-largest.

## Contextual relativity

12. **The ambiguous intervals are resolved only by surroundings.** The tritone sounds neutral in chromatic passages, restless in diatonic ones (Ex. 1-2). The perfect fourth sounds consonant in dissonant surroundings and dissonant in consonant ones (Ex. 1-3).
13. **Perception is relative to the local norm.** Both cases work by contrast with what's around them — an interval is heard by its distance from the prevailing tension level, not by an absolute measure.
14. **Order of approach determines character.** The same ambiguous tritone that reads restless when tension is rising reads neutral when it arrives at the end of a decrescendo of tension (Ex. 1-5 vs. Ex. 1-6). Identical interval, opposite meaning, because of what preceded it.

## Tension as a designed parameter

15. **Tension is compositional material you can shape.** Intervals may be arranged in any order to form any pattern of tension interplay — low-to-high, high-to-low, or any other curve.
16. **The abstract plan precedes the music.** Ex. 1-4 gives a bare tension scheme; Ex. 1-5 is that scheme realized in rhythm, register, and texture. The tension curve is a structural layer separate from its surface setting.
17. **Repose is as valid a destination as tension.** Ex. 1-6 reverses the arrow and ends in relative rest, which the *rit. e dim.* reinforces.
18. **Tension serves design and function** — it is subordinate to the form, not an end in itself.

## Interaction with the other forces

19. **Intervallic tension is one force among several** — timbre, dynamics, and tempo are peers, not accompaniments.
20. **Those forces may agree or disagree with the intervals.** Sharp dissonances in loud double reeds agree (raucous); the same intervals in muted strings disagree (introspective). Ex. 1-7 stages exactly this: Allegro/oboes/*f* labeled agreement, Lento/muted strings/*p* labeled disagreement — with the intervallic content held constant so only the setting varies.
21. **Neither strategy is superior.** Agreement and disagreement are both expressive options, chosen for effect.

## Historical relativity

22. **The consonance/dissonance concept itself is historically mobile.** As composers' attitudes and practices change, so does the concept; degrees of tension formerly heard as dissonant may be accepted as consonance.
23. **Consonance is a norm, not an absolute.** In a harmony built from strongly dissonant intervals, those dissonances *become* the consonant norm — and genuinely consonant intervals may then sound dissonant against them. This generalizes the perfect-fourth principle to the whole system.
24. **The classification in Ex. 1-1 is therefore a starting reference, not a law** — and the running head on the last page ("Inversion and Spacing") signals that even the physical taxonomy gets qualified further by register and voicing.

## Inversion

1. **Inversion changes an interval's consonant-dissonant quality**, because inverting alters spacing and register — the pitch classes stay, the sound does not.
2. **The amount of change is interval-specific.** There's no uniform rule; each interval must be judged individually.
3. **Inversion can change an interval's functional class outright.** The P5, a solid open consonance, becomes the unstable P4. This is the exception that qualifies the tidy inversion-pairing of Ex. 1-1.
4. **Sharp dissonances change most in intensity.** The narrow, cutting minor second opens out into a widespreading major seventh and loses some of its sting — same dissonance class, very different bite.
5. **Narrowness produces bite; width produces spread.** The change is a function of physical distance between the tones, not of category.
6. **The tritone is the special case: register shifts, distance doesn't.** Inverting it moves it in register while the intervallic span (six semitones) is unchanged — the one interval whose inversion is purely a matter of placement.

## Spacing beyond the octave

7. **Spacing is an independent tension variable**, ranking alongside the interval's own identity. Wide spacing recolors every class:
   - Soft consonances (3rds, 6ths) become **richer**.
   - Open consonances (octaves, 5ths) and the consonant P4 become **stronger**.
   - Dissonances (2nds, 7ths) become **less biting yet more brilliant**.
8. **Wide spacing trades bite for brilliance.** Tension isn't reduced so much as converted — the harshness leaves, the vividness stays.
9. **Wide spacing loosens the tritone's pull toward resolution.** Its neutral chromatic form becomes more ambiguous and veiled; its restless diatonic form becomes even less addicted to resolution. Both readings drift further from functional obligation.
10. **The ambiguous intervals stay ambiguous under spacing** — spacing intensifies their existing character rather than resolving it.

## What a chord is, and how it's built

11. **Two or more simultaneous intervals form what is "usually felt to be" a chord** — the hedge is deliberate; chordhood is perceptual, not definitional.
12. **A chord is a collection of intervals**, not a stack of thirds with a root. This is the pivot of the whole chapter.
13. **Three construction methods, all equally available:**
    - **Equidistant** — one interval repeated (Ex. 1-11 does this for every interval from m2 through M7).
    - **Same interval number, different quality** — major and minor 2nds together, perfect and augmented 4ths, etc.
    - **Mixed intervals** — free combination.
14. **Tertian construction is one option among twelve**, not the default. Quartal, quintal, secundal, and seventh-based chords are structurally equivalent choices.

## Analyzing chords by interval content

15. **Before setting a chord in motion, note the quality of every interval it contains.** Analysis precedes progression.
16. **Harmonic tension can only be controlled through differences in chordal values as determined by interval characteristics.** This is stated as the sole means.
17. **Without this, only limited progression is available** — the kind produced by root relationships within a fixed scale and key. Intervallic awareness buys flexibility that functional harmony can't.
18. **Good part-writing is required to realize that flexibility.** Freedom of material doesn't excuse voice-leading craft.
19. **Interval count grows combinatorially:** 3 notes = 3 intervals, 4 notes = 6, 5 notes = 10. (n(n−1)/2.) Density of relationships rises much faster than note count.
20. **A chord can be described as a census of its intervals** — "3 cons., 2 sharp, 1 neutral diss." This is a tension profile, not a label.
21. **Chords form a continuous tension gradient** (Ex. 1-14 runs three-note chords from 3 consonances to 1 mild + 2 sharp dissonances), parallel to the interval gradient of Ex. 1-1.

## The two-axis classification

22. **Chordal tension both affects and is affected by dynamics, medium, and spacing**, and varies by context — the same reciprocity established for intervals in Ex. 1-7.
23. **A general classification makes the material manageable.** Persichetti offers taxonomy as a practical tool, not a truth claim.
24. **Axis one: does the chord contain at least one sharp dissonance?** Axis two: **does it contain at least one tritone?** Four classes result (Ex. 1-16).
25. **The two axes are independent.** Sharp dissonance governs harshness; the tritone governs restlessness.
26. **Dissonance and instability are not the same thing.** Tritone chords are restless; tritone-free chords are stable *even when extremely dissonant*. You can write a violently dissonant chord that sits still.
27. **A single instance of either element is enough to classify** — "at least one." These are presence tests, not proportions.

## The perfect fourth in chords

28. **A P4 makes a chord ambiguous**, because the interval itself can go either way.
29. **A chord containing a P4 is classifiable only in its total interval context** — the other intervals decide.
30. **The specific test:** look at the interval formed between the bass note and the note *not* part of the fourth.
    - If that interval is a **mild or sharp dissonance** → the P4 sounds like an **open consonance**.
    - If that interval is a **soft consonance** → the P4 sounds **mildly dissonant**.
31. **This is the earlier contrast principle made operational.** The P4 reads as opposite to its surroundings; here the "surroundings" are precisely identified as one measurable interval, turning an aesthetic observation into a usable procedure.

## Doubling

32. **Every chord has a "natural" doubling under normal conditions** — roots in major triads, for instance. The quotation marks around "normal" concede the norm is conventional.
33. **Any note may be doubled, tripled, or omitted** for textural purposes. Doubling is a color decision, not a rule-following one.
34. **Three purposes:** enriching simple chords, pointing up characteristic part-writing, strengthening particular parts of a chord.
35. **Doubling amplifies whatever it duplicates.** A doubled major third adds color; a doubled dissonant tone increases the bite. The doubled tone's own quality determines the effect.
36. **Excessive coupling — all voices doubled — produces percussive harmony.** At the limit, doubling stops being a harmonic device and becomes a timbral/rhythmic one.

## Spacing, clarity, and the overtone series

37. **Chord members can be arranged so that specific interval types monopolize the pattern** — voicing selects which intervals dominate the ear.
38. **Voicing produces clouded or clear harmony** — Ex. 1-20 labels alternative spacings (a) and (b) of the same material.
39. **Clouded harmony may remain clouded and still be effective.** Muddiness is not an error state to be corrected.
40. **Both cloudy and clear materials are essential ingredients.** Neither is the default or the goal; the pair is a resource.
41. **The overtone series is the measuring stick for the aural difference.** Spacing that follows the series — wide at the bottom, closer above — reads clear; spacing that contradicts it, especially close intervals low down, reads clouded. The same acoustic fact that grounded the consonance table now governs voicing.
42. **The chapter's method is consistent throughout:** state a physical basis, then show that context, register, spacing, doubling, and medium can override it — and treat that overridability as compositional opportunity rather than as a defect in the theory.
