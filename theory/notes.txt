# keys
- A key organizes music around one central note.
- In C major, C is the central note.
- The other notes get their functions from how they relate to C.
- Calling C the central note is only a starting point.
- To understand key, we must study the specific relationships between C and the other notes.

# tonic
- The tonic is the main note of a key.
- Melodies can make us expect a return to the tonic.
- The tonic can feel like a destination.
- Delaying that return creates tension.

# scales
- A key is built mainly from a specific group of notes.
- A scale arranges those notes in pitch order.
- The basic scale of a key begins and ends on the tonic.
- Each note in the scale has a scale degree number.
- The scale degrees are also named:
  1 = tonic
  2 = supertonic
  3 = mediant
  4 = subdominant
  5 = dominant
  6 = submediant
  7 = leading tone

# octaves
- An *octave* is the distance between one note and the next higher or lower note with the same letter name.
- Notes an octave apart differ in pitch height but still sound closely related.
- *Octave equivalence* is the idea that notes separated by octaves are treated as versions of the same note.
- A *register* is the part of the pitch range in which a note occurs.
- Register notation tells us which high or low version of a note is meant.
- The same letter name can therefore refer to several pitches in different registers.
- *Octave* = same note name, higher or lower
- *Octave equivalence* = different pitch height, same basic note identity
- *Register* = where the note sits in the overall high-to-low range

# scale
- A *scale* moves through notes in order from one pitch to the next.
- The distance from one scale note to the next is called a *step*.
- A major scale uses two step sizes.
- A *half step* (*semitone*) is the smaller distance.
- A *whole step* (*whole tone*) equals two half steps.
- A *major scale* follows the pattern W–W–H–W–W–W–H.
- This places the half steps between scale degrees 3–4 and 7–8.
- A *diatonic scale* is a seven-note scale built from five whole steps and two half steps.
- The major scale is one kind of diatonic scale.
- Different diatonic scales place the two half steps in different positions.
- W = whole step
- H = half step
- Major scale = W–W–H–W–W–W–H

# intervals
- An *interval* is the relationship between two notes.
- If the notes sound at the same time, the interval is *vertical* or *harmonic*.
- If the notes sound one after the other, the interval is *horizontal* or *melodic*.
- The basic size of an interval is found by counting letter names from one note to the other, including both notes.
- The number of letter names gives the interval number: 2 letters = 2nd, 3 = 3rd, 4 = 4th, and so on.
- An interval spanning eight letter names is called an *octave*, not an eighth.
- The interval number does not tell the exact pitch distance.
- Two intervals with the same number can have different sizes in half steps.

# chords
- A *chord* is a group of three or more notes heard as one musical unit.
- A chord can be presented with all its notes sounding at once.
- A *block chord* has all chord tones sounding at the same time.
- A chord can also be spread out so its notes sound one after another.
- A *broken chord* or *arpeggio* presents chord tones one after another while still forming one chord.
- A *triad* is a three-note chord built from a root, a 3rd above it, and a 5th above it.
- The *root* is the note that gives the triad its basic identity.
- If the root is the tonic, the chord is the *tonic triad* or *tonic chord*.

# active tones; stable tones
- Music often creates a sense that one note is moving toward another.
- This motion comes partly from how notes function within a key.
- *Stable tones* sound like points of rest or arrival.
- *Active tones* sound less settled and tend to move toward more stable tones.
- The tonic, scale degree 1, is the most stable tone and the strongest goal.
- Scale degrees 3 and 5 can also sound stable because they belong to the tonic triad.
- Motion to 3 or 5 can feel settled, but less final than motion to 1.
- A melody can begin on 3 or 5 and still be in the tonic key.
- The melodic starting note does not have to be the tonic.

# passive tones; neighboring tones
- *Stable tones* act as points of rest, while *active tones* tend to move toward them.
- In a major key, scale degrees 1, 3, 5, and 8 can function as stable tones.
- Active tones can connect or decorate these stable tones.
- A *passing tone* fills the stepwise space between two different stable tones.
- Passing tones create smooth motion from one stable tone to another.
- A *neighboring tone* moves one step away from a stable tone and then returns to it.
- An *upper neighbor* moves up by step and returns.
- A *lower neighbor* moves down by step and returns.
- Passing and neighboring tones can receive rhythmic emphasis, but their tendency toward a stable tone still shapes how they are heard.
- A melodic line can connect stable tones even when other notes occur between them.
- P = passing tone
- N = neighboring tone
- UN = upper neighbor
- LN = lower neighbor

# half steps as melod intensifiers
- An *active tone* tends to move toward a more *stable tone*.
- That pull becomes stronger when the two notes are only a half step apart.
- Half-step motion therefore makes a melodic goal feel more intense and direct.
- In a major key, scale degree 4 is strongly drawn to 3.
- Scale degree 7 is strongly drawn to 8, or the tonic.
- Scale degree 7 is called the *leading tone* because it strongly “leads” into the tonic.
- These half-step attractions help make the tonic and the key feel clearly established.

# incomplete neighbors; double neighbors
- A *neighboring tone* normally forms a three-note pattern: stable tone → neighbor → same stable tone.
- An *incomplete neighbor* omits one of those two connections to the stable tone.
- It may begin on the stable tone and move away: stable → neighbor.
- Or it may approach the stable tone from a neighboring note: neighbor → stable.
- The neighboring note is still understood in relation to that stable tone, even though the full away-and-back pattern is missing.
- A *double neighbor* surrounds one stable tone with both its upper and lower neighbors.
- Its basic shape is: stable → upper/lower neighbor → lower/upper neighbor → stable.
- The two neighbors can appear in either order.
- IN = incomplete neighbor
- DN = double neighbor

# transposition; key signatures
- A *recapitulation* is the return of earlier material near the end of a sonata-form movement.
- A musical idea can return in a different key.
- Moving a piece or section into another key is called *transposing*.
- Transposition keeps the same musical relationships while changing the pitch level.
- In a major key, the notes must still follow the major-scale pattern W–W–H–W–W–W–H.
- Sharps or flats are added as needed to preserve that pattern.
- A *key signature* collects the sharps or flats that normally belong to a key and places them at the beginning of the staff.
- An *accidental* is a sharp, flat, or natural written beside a note when a pitch differs from the key signature.
- Major keys with sharps progress upward by 5ths.
- Major keys with flats progress downward by 5ths.
- Sharp-key sequence: C → G → D → A → E → B → F♯ → C♯
- Flat-key sequence: C → F → B♭ → E♭ → A♭ → D♭ → G♭ → C♭

# chromaticism; chromatic half steps
- *Chromaticism* is the use of notes that do not normally belong to the current key.
- These outside notes often decorate or strengthen motion within the key rather than change the key itself.
- A *chromatic passing tone* is an outside note that fills the space between two notes by step.
- Chromatic passing tones often make melodic motion feel stronger because they create half-step movement.
- A *chromatic half step* occurs between two notes with the same letter name, such as F–F♯.
- A *diatonic half step* occurs between two notes with different letter names, such as B–C.
- A chromatic passing tone can divide a whole step into two half steps.
- The chromatic note is usually spelled with the same letter name as the diatonic note it alters.
- Not every chromatic note creates a chromatic half step.
- Chromatic notes can also use double sharps or double flats when the spelling requires them.
- Chromatic passing motion: F → F♯ → G
- Chromatic half step: F → F♯
- Diatonic half step: B → C

# enharmonics
- Keyboard instruments commonly use *equal temperament*.
- *Equal temperament* divides the octave into 12 equal half steps.
- This tuning makes some differently named notes use the same piano key and sound at the same pitch.
- Notes with different names but the same pitch are called *enharmonic equivalents*.
- C♯ and D♭ are enharmonic equivalents.
- G♯ and A♭ are enharmonic equivalents.
- The different spellings still matter because they show how a note functions within a key or musical line.

# minor keys
- A *minor key* is organized around a tonic just as a major key is.
- The key signature alone does not tell us which note is the tonic.
- The tonic is identified by how the music centers, begins, ends, and forms its tonic chord.
- Once the tonic is known, the quality of the tonic triad distinguishes major from minor.
- The tonic triad contains scale degrees 1, 3, and 5.
- Major and minor tonic triads keep 1 and 5 the same but change scale degree 3.
- A *minor key* has a minor 3rd between scale degrees 1 and 3.
- A *major key* has a major 3rd between scale degrees 1 and 3.
- The minor 3rd is smaller than the major 3rd.
- Minor tonic triad = 1–♭3–5
- Major tonic triad = 1–3–5

# the natural form of minor
- The *natural minor scale* uses the notes of the minor key signature without chromatic changes.
- Its defining feature is a minor 3rd between scale degrees 1 and 3.
- Its half steps fall between 2–3 and 5–6.
- Its step pattern is W–H–W–W–H–W–W.
- Scale degrees 1, 3, 5, and 8 can function as stable tones.
- Scale degree 7 lies a whole step below the tonic, so it does not pull strongly toward 8.
- For this reason, natural minor scale degree 7 is called the *subtonic*, not the *leading tone*.
- This weak pull is usually fine when the melody descends because 7 is moving away from the tonic.
- When the melody rises toward the tonic, composers often raise 7 by a half step.
- Raising 7 creates the strong half-step motion 7–8 and makes the tonic sound more clearly like a goal.
- Natural minor: 1–2–♭3–4–5–♭6–♭7–8
- Half steps: 2–3 and 5–6

# harmonic form of minor
- The *harmonic minor scale* begins with the natural minor scale and raises scale degree 7.
- Raising 7 creates a *leading tone* a half step below the tonic.
- This gives 7 a strong pull toward 8 and supports important harmonic progressions.
- The rest of the natural minor scale remains unchanged.
- The raised 7 creates a large gap between scale degrees 6 and 7.
- That gap is an *augmented 2nd*, equal to three half steps.
- Because of this gap, harmonic minor can sound less smooth in stepwise melodies.
- Harmonic minor has half steps between 2–3, 5–6, and 7–8.
- In some sharp keys, raising 7 requires a double sharp.
- Harmonic minor: 1–2–♭3–4–5–♭6–7–8
- Step pattern: W–H–W–W–H–A2–H

# the melodic form of minor
- The *melodic minor scale* solves a melodic problem created by harmonic minor.
- Harmonic minor raises scale degree 7, which creates a large gap between 6 and 7.
- Melodic minor raises scale degree 6 as well as 7 when the scale ascends.
- Raising 6 removes the augmented 2nd and creates smoother stepwise motion toward the tonic.
- The ascending melodic minor scale therefore has half steps between 2–3 and 7–8.
- When the scale descends, 6 and 7 usually return to their natural-minor forms.
- Ascending melodic minor: 1–2–♭3–4–5–6–7–8
- Descending melodic minor: 8–♭7–♭6–5–4–♭3–2–1

# the three forms of minor
- Natural, harmonic, and melodic minor are not three unrelated scales.
- *Natural minor* is the basic form.
- *Harmonic minor* is natural minor with scale degree 7 raised.
- *Melodic minor* changes scale degrees 6 and 7 to create smoother melodic motion.
- Music in a minor key commonly uses all three forms as needed.
- Harmony often determines when harmonic minor appears.
- Melodic direction often determines when melodic minor appears.
- Ascending lines often use raised 6 and 7.
- Descending lines often return to the natural minor form.

# key signatures in minor
- Minor keys use key signatures just as major keys do.
- A minor has no sharps or flats.
- Moving upward by 5ths adds one sharp at each new key.
- Moving downward by 5ths adds one flat at each new key.
- These accidentals preserve the whole-step and half-step pattern of the natural minor scale.
- Sharp-side minor keys: A → E → B → F♯ → C♯ → G♯ → D♯ → A♯
- Flat-side minor keys: A → D → G → C → F → B♭ → E♭ → A♭

# relative major and minor
- *Relative major* and *relative minor* are a major key and a minor key that share the same key signature.
- They use the same collection of notes but have different tonics.
- Because the tonic changes, relative major and minor are different keys.
- The tonic of the relative minor is scale degree 6 of the major key.
- The tonic of the relative major is scale degree 3 of the minor key.
- Major → relative minor: go to scale degree 6.
- Minor → relative major: go to scale degree 3.

# parallel major and minor; mixture
- *Parallel major* and *parallel minor* are major and minor keys that share the same tonic.
- Because they share the same tonic, they feel more closely related than relative major and minor.
- Their key signatures are different because major and minor use different scale patterns.
- *Mixture* is the use of notes or chords from the parallel major or parallel minor within the current key.
- In minor, raising scale degrees 6 and 7 can create notes borrowed from the parallel major.
- In practice, some theoretical parallel keys are respelled enharmonically to avoid awkward key signatures with double flats or double sharps.

# modes; the diatonic order
- A *mode* is a scale pattern organized around a particular tonic.
- The seven diatonic modes all use seven notes and contain five whole steps and two half steps.
- What changes from one mode to another is where those whole steps and half steps occur.
- Changing that pattern changes the way the scale is organized around its tonic.
- The seven modes together form the *diatonic order*.
- The seven modes are Ionian, Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian, and Locrian.
- *Ionian* is the same basic scale form as major.
- *Aeolian* is the same basic scale form as natural minor.
- In Baroque through Romantic tonal music, major and minor are the main modes, though other modes can still appear.
- Diatonic modes: Ionian → Dorian → Phrygian → Lydian → Mixolydian → Aeolian → Locrian
- A mode is a diatonic scale built from a tonic.
- All seven diatonic modes use 7 notes, 5 whole steps, and 2 half steps.
- What changes is the placement of the half steps.
- Ionian = major
- Aeolian = natural minor
- Ionian: 1 2 3 4 5 6 7
- Dorian: 1 2 ♭3 4 5 6 ♭7
- Phrygian: 1 ♭2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7
- Lydian: 1 2 3 ♯4 5 6 7
- Mixolydian: 1 2 3 4 5 6 ♭7
- Aeolian: 1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7
- Locrian: 1 ♭2 ♭3 4 ♭5 ♭6 ♭7
- Order: Ionian → Dorian → Phrygian → Lydian → Mixolydian → Aeolian → Locrian
- Major-family reference:
- Major = Ionian
- Minor = Aeolian

# tonality
- *Tonal* music is organized around a central tone.
- *Tonality* is the system that organizes the other tones around that central tone.
- Many kinds of music can be tonal, including major, minor, modal, and other tonal systems.
- What differs between these systems is how the other tones relate to the tonal center.
- *Major-minor tonality* is the tonal system based specifically on major and minor keys.
- In this context, *tonal* and *tonality* often refer specifically to major-minor tonality.

# the contrast between major and minor
- Major and minor often carry different emotional associations.
- Major is often heard as brighter or more cheerful.
- Minor is often heard as darker or sadder.
- These associations are not absolute.
- A piece’s emotional character also depends on rhythm, melody, harmony, tempo, texture, and context.
- Major music can sound solemn, and minor music can sound light or playful.
- Even so, the choice between *major* and *minor* can strongly affect musical character.
- The association between mode and emotion has existed in Western music theory for centuries.
- The source suggests that these associations may come partly from the tonal relationships themselves, not only from cultural habit.

# recognizing and constructing intervals: numerical size and quality
- An *interval* is identified by two things: its *numerical size* and its *quality*.
- The *numerical size* tells how many letter names the interval spans, counting both notes.
- The *quality* tells the exact size of that interval in half steps.
- Intervals can be *horizontal* (*melodic*) when the notes sound one after another, or *vertical* (*harmonic*) when they sound together.
- Interval qualities are *major* (M), *minor* (m), *perfect* (P), *augmented* (A), and *diminished* (d).
- Intervals fall into two groups because they use different quality systems.
- Unisons, 4ths, 5ths, and octaves use *perfect* as their basic quality.
- 2nds, 3rds, 6ths, and 7ths use *major* and *minor* as their basic qualities.
- For unisons, 4ths, 5ths, and octaves: perfect → one half step larger = augmented; perfect → one half step smaller = diminished.
- For 2nds, 3rds, 6ths, and 7ths: major → one half step larger = augmented; major → one half step smaller = minor; minor → one half step smaller = diminished.
- A useful reference is the major scale built on the lower note.
- If the upper note belongs to that major scale, 1, 4, 5, and 8 are perfect; 2, 3, 6, and 7 are major.
- When building an interval, determine the letter name first so the numerical size stays correct.
- Then adjust the accidental to produce the required quality.
- Raising the upper note makes an interval larger; lowering it makes the interval smaller.
- Raising the lower note makes an interval smaller; lowering it makes the interval larger.
- Perfect group: 1, 4, 5, 8 → d–P–A
- Major/minor group: 2, 3, 6, 7 → d–m–M–A

# recognizing and constructing intervals: compound intervals
- A *compound interval* is any interval larger than an octave.
- Compound intervals keep the same basic interval identity as their simpler form within one octave.
- Subtracting 7 from a compound interval number gives its simple equivalent.
- A 9th is a compound 2nd.
- A 10th is a compound 3rd.
- An 11th is a compound 4th.
- A 12th is a compound 5th.
- A 15th is a compound octave.
- In practice, many compound intervals are referred to by their simpler names.
- 9th → 2nd
- 10th → 3rd
- 11th → 4th
- 12th → 5th
- 15th → octave

# recognizing and constructing intervals: interval inversion
- *Interval inversion* means moving one note of an interval by an octave so the lower note becomes the upper note, or the upper note becomes the lower note.
- The inverted interval keeps the same two pitch classes but reverses their positions.
- An interval and its inversion always add up to 9 by numerical size.
- This gives the pairs: 1st ↔ 8th, 2nd ↔ 7th, 3rd ↔ 6th, 4th ↔ 5th.
- Inversion also changes interval quality in a predictable way.
- *Perfect* remains *perfect*.
- *Major* becomes *minor*, and *minor* becomes *major*.
- *Augmented* becomes *diminished*, and *diminished* becomes *augmented*.
- M3 ↔ m6
- m3 ↔ M6
- P4 ↔ P5
- A4 ↔ d5

# the overtone series: composite sounds and overtones
- Most musical tones are *composite sounds*: one heard tone contains several vibrations at once.
- The main vibration gives the pitch we hear and is called the *fundamental tone*.
- At the same time, parts of the vibrating body can vibrate independently and produce higher tones called *overtones*.
- The fundamental and its overtones blend together into a single sound.
- The relative strength of those overtones helps determine *timbre*, or tone color.
- Their *intensity* means how loud or strong each overtone is.
- Different instruments can play the same pitch but sound different because their overtones have different intensities.
- The overtones occur in a regular pattern called the *overtone series*.
- Each frequency in that series is called a *partial*.
- The fundamental is the 1st partial.
- The 1st overtone is the 2nd partial.
- The 2nd overtone is the 3rd partial, and so on.
- Higher partials become progressively closer together in pitch and are usually weaker.

- Partial 1 = fundamental
- Partial 2 = octave above the fundamental
- Partial 3 = perfect 5th above that octave
- Partial 4 = two octaves above the fundamental
- Partial 5 ≈ major 3rd above partial 4
- Partial 6 ≈ perfect 5th above partial 4
- The series continues upward with increasingly small intervals between neighboring partials.

# the overtone series and the tonal system
- The *overtone series* contains several intervals and chords that are important in tonal music.
- Its lower partials naturally produce the octave, perfect 5th, major 3rd, and major triad.
- These relationships resemble some of the most stable structures in major-minor tonality.
- The tonal system does not simply copy the overtone series.
- Some overtones do not fit ordinary major or minor scales, and some important tonal structures, such as the minor triad, do not arise directly from the series.
- The overtone series therefore does not provide a complete explanation of tonal harmony.
- It does, however, parallel several common features of tonal music.
- Major triads often function as especially stable chords.
- Perfect 5ths form especially close tonal relationships.
- Octaves, 5ths, and 3rds are among the most stable intervals.
- A triad is understood as growing from its *root*, much as overtones arise above a fundamental.
- These similarities show a strong correspondence between acoustics and tonal practice, but they do not prove that the overtone series caused the tonal system.

# consonance and dissonance: stable and unstable intervals
- *Consonance* describes intervals that sound stable or at rest.
- *Dissonance* describes intervals that sound unstable, active, or tense.
- Here, these terms apply mainly to *vertical intervals*, where both notes sound at the same time.
- Perfect unisons, perfect octaves, and perfect 5ths are consonant.
- Major and minor 3rds and 6ths are consonant.
- 2nds and 7ths are dissonant.
- Augmented and diminished intervals are dissonant.
- The perfect 4th can be consonant or dissonant depending on context.
- Consonant: P1, P8, P5, M3, m3, M6, m6
- Dissonant: all 2nds, all 7ths, all augmented intervals, all diminished intervals
- Context-dependent: P4

# consonance and dissonance: the consonant intervals
- Consonant intervals do not all feel equally stable.
- The *perfect unison* and *perfect octave* are the most stable because the two notes are identical or differ only by octave.
- The *perfect 5th* is the next most stable and strongly supports the identity of a chord root and key.
- Major and minor 3rds are less stable than the 5th but define the quality of major and minor triads.
- The major 3rd is generally treated as more stable than the minor 3rd.
- Major and minor 6ths are consonant but more active than 3rds and 5ths.
- The *perfect 4th* is special because it can function as either consonant or dissonant depending on context.
- Relative stability: P1/P8 → P5 → M3 → m3 → M6/m6 → P4

# consonance and dissonance: perfect and imperfect consonances
- Consonances can be divided into *perfect consonances* and *imperfect consonances*.
- *Perfect consonances* are the unison, octave, and perfect 5th.
- These intervals sound more stable and are often used at important points of arrival.
- *Imperfect consonances* are major and minor 3rds and 6ths.
- These intervals are still consonant, but they sound more active and flexible.
- Because of this, imperfect consonances are often used while the music is moving from one stable point to another.
- In two-part writing, perfect consonances are favored at beginnings and endings, while imperfect consonances are common in the middle.
- Perfect consonances: P1, P5, P8
- Imperfect consonances: M3, m3, M6, m6

# consonance and dissonance: the dissonant intervals
- *Dissonant intervals* create tension between two parts rather than acting as stable chord tones.
- In tonal music, this tension often comes from melodic motion in one or both parts.
- *Passing tones* create dissonance while moving by step between stable tones.
- *Neighboring tones* create dissonance while stepping away from a stable tone and returning.
- Dissonance works most clearly when the melody approaches it by step and leaves it by step.
- This stepwise motion connects the dissonance closely to the surrounding consonances.
- The resulting tension gives the melody a sense of direction toward a more stable interval.
- *Isolated dissonances* lack this clear connection and can sound directionless.
- Common pattern: consonance → step → dissonance → step → consonance

# consonance and dissonance: dissonance and activity
- *Active tones* tend to move toward more stable tones.
- In a major key, scale degrees 2, 4, 6, and 7 are normally active.
- These tones often form *dissonant intervals* against the stable tones of the tonic chord: 1, 3, and 5.
- This connects melodic activity with harmonic dissonance.
- Consonant support can make an active tone sound more stable without removing its tendency to move.
- A normally stable tone can also become unstable if it forms a dissonance with another part.
- Stability therefore depends on both the scale degree and the interval it forms with the surrounding music.
- The strongest sense of rest comes when the music reaches the tonic.

# consonance and dissonance: P4
- The *perfect 4th* is unusual because it can function as either consonant or dissonant.
- Earlier music often treated the perfect 4th as a stable consonance.
- As triadic harmony became more important, the 4th often began to sound active rather than stable.
- A 4th tends to sound dissonant when it behaves like an upper note that wants to move down to a 3rd.
- A 4th tends to sound consonant when it functions clearly as the inversion of a stable 5th.
- In simple two-part writing, the 4th is usually treated as dissonant.
- In thicker textures, a 4th above the bass is also often treated as dissonant.
- A 4th can sound consonant when the surrounding harmony makes it part of a stable sonority.
- P4 = context-dependent

# intervals in a key: intervals between scale degrees
- A *scale degree* gets part of its character from the intervals it forms with the other notes of the scale.
- Changing those interval relationships changes how that scale degree sounds and functions.
- Major and minor differ partly because the tonic forms different intervals with scale degrees 3, 6, and 7.
- In major, the tonic forms a major 3rd, major 6th, and major 7th with 3, 6, and 7.
- In natural minor, the tonic forms a minor 3rd, minor 6th, and minor 7th with 3, 6, and 7.
- These interval differences help give major and minor their different tonal character.
- Within a *diatonic scale*, every scale degree forms its own pattern of intervals with the other scale degrees.
- That interval pattern helps give each scale degree its own identity.
- Two scale degrees can have similar interval patterns without being identical.
- Diatonic scales produce only certain interval qualities: perfect unisons and octaves; perfect or diminished 5ths; perfect or augmented 4ths; major or minor 2nds, 3rds, 6ths, and 7ths.

# intervals in a key: the diminished 5th and the augmented 4th in major
- In a major key, scale degrees 4 and 7 are both active tones.
- Scale degree 4 tends to move down to 3, while scale degree 7 tends to move up to 1.
- When 4 and 7 sound together, they form a strong dissonance.
- If 7 is below 4, the interval is a *diminished 5th*.
- If 4 is below 7, the interval is an *augmented 4th*.
- These are two forms of the same tension between scale degrees 4 and 7.
- A *resolution* is the motion of a dissonant interval into a more stable consonance.
- The diminished 5th resolves inward: 7 → 1 and 4 → 3.
- The augmented 4th resolves outward: 4 → 3 and 7 → 1.
- Both resolutions lead to scale degrees 1 and 3, which belong to the tonic triad.
- This motion strongly points toward the tonic and helps identify the key.
- A *tritone* traditionally means an augmented 4th spanning three whole steps; the term is also commonly used for the diminished 5th.
- d5: 7–4 → 1–3
- A4: 4–7 → 3–1

# intervals in a key: the diminished 5th and the augmented 4th in minor
- Natural minor contains a *diminished 5th* between scale degrees 2 and 6, or an *augmented 4th* when the notes are reversed.
- This tritone resolves to scale degrees 3 and 5.
- Although 3 and 5 belong to the tonic triad, they do not identify the tonic strongly by themselves.
- Without scale degree 1, scale degree 3 can sound like the root of another triad.
- Natural minor therefore does not define its tonic as strongly through this tritone as major does.
- Raising scale degree 7 creates a new tritone between 4 and 7.
- This tritone resolves to 3 and 1, directly pointing to the tonic triad.
- Harmonic and melodic minor use this raised 7 to strengthen the sense of key.
- Ascending melodic minor can also create a tritone between raised 6 and 3, but this has little importance for defining the key.
- Natural minor tritone: 2–6 → 3–5
- Raised-7 tritone: 4–7 → 3–1

# intervals in a key: the diminished 7th and the augmented 2nd
- In harmonic minor, raised scale degree 7 and natural scale degree 6 form a *diminished 7th*.
- When inverted, the same two scale degrees form an *augmented 2nd*.
- Both intervals are dissonant and tend to resolve.
- The diminished 7th resolves to scale degrees 1 and 5, forming a perfect 5th.
- The augmented 2nd also resolves to 1 and 5, but as a perfect 4th.
- Because a perfect 5th is normally stable, the diminished 7th resolves more freely.
- Because a perfect 4th can be dissonant, the augmented 2nd is used more carefully.
- These intervals have strong *key-defining* power because they occur specifically between natural 6 and raised 7 in minor.
- Their resolution to 1 and 5 strongly points to the tonic.
- A diminished 7th can also appear in major through *mixture*, by borrowing scale degree ♭6 from the parallel minor.
- d7: 7–6 → 1–5
- A2: 6–7 → 5–1

# the remaining intervals
- Some less common intervals arise when minor scales or chromatic notes alter the usual diatonic pitches.
- In minor, scale degree 3 combined with raised 7 can form an *augmented 5th* or, when inverted, a *diminished 4th*.
- Chromatic alteration can also create a *diminished 3rd* or, when inverted, an *augmented 6th*.
- One common source is raised scale degree 4 combined with natural scale degree 6.
- Raised 4 often acts as an active chromatic tone that moves toward 5.
- These unusual intervals often result from melodic motion rather than from a new harmonic structure.
- Other augmented or diminished intervals can appear as temporary by-products of chromatic decoration.
- A5 ↔ d4
- A6 ↔ d3

# enharmonically equivalent intervals
- *Enharmonically equivalent intervals* are intervals that sound the same in equal temperament but are spelled differently.
- Because the notes are spelled differently, the intervals can have different numerical sizes and different qualities.
- The sounding pitches may match, but the musical meaning does not have to match.
- In isolation, two enharmonically equivalent intervals are hard or impossible to distinguish by ear.
- In musical context, their spelling can make them function differently.
- This is because notation shows how each note relates to the key, the harmony, and the melodic motion.
- An interval’s name therefore depends on spelling, not only on sound.
- Same sound does not always mean same interval.
- Common enharmonic pairs:
- m3 = A2
- A4 = d5
- M6 = d7
- A6 = m7
- Rule:
- Same sounding pitch pair + different spelling = enharmonically equivalent intervals
- Different spelling can change:
- interval number
- interval quality
- harmonic function

# rhythmic organization: the beat
- *Rhythm* organizes music in time through duration, accent, and grouping.
- The basic unit of rhythmic time is the *beat* or *pulse*.
- A beat is a regularly recurring span of time that provides a framework for rhythm.
- Notes can last longer than a beat, match the beat, or divide the beat into smaller durations.
- Note values can be divided into smaller equal parts or combined into larger values.
- The basic note-value hierarchy divides by two: whole → half → quarter → eighth → sixteenth → thirty-second → sixty-fourth.
- 1 whole note = 2 half notes = 4 quarter notes = 8 eighth notes = 16 sixteenth notes = 32 thirty-second notes = 64 sixty-fourth notes.
- Rests use the same duration relationships as notes.
- A *dot* adds half of a note or rest’s original value.
- Each additional dot adds half the value added by the previous dot.
- Dotted note = original + 1/2
- Double-dotted note = original + 1/2 + 1/4
- Triple-dotted note = original + 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8
- A beat or duration can also be divided into other equal groupings called *tuplets*.
- A *triplet* divides a duration into three equal parts where two of the same note value would normally fit.
- Other tuplets can divide a duration into groups such as 5 or 7.
- The number written over a tuplet identifies the number of notes in the grouping.
- 3 = triplet
- 5 = quintuplet
- 7 = septuplet
- A *tie* joins notes of the same pitch so they sound as one continuous duration.
- The duration of tied notes equals the sum of their written values.
- Ties allow durations to cross beats or bar lines and can make rhythmic grouping easier to see.

# tempo
- *Tempo* is the pace of the beat.
- A fast tempo means the beats occur quickly; a slow tempo means they occur slowly.
- Tempo is determined by the beat, not by the fastest notes in the music.
- A slow-tempo passage can contain many fast notes between its beats without changing the tempo.
- Tempo affects the overall rate of musical motion, including how quickly harmonies and melodies progress.
- Written note values show durations relative to one another, not fixed amounts of time.
- A quarter note always lasts twice as long as an eighth note and half as long as a half note.
- The actual duration of those notes depends on the tempo.

# accent
- *Accent* means emphasis.
- An accented note stands out more than the notes around it.
- A performer can create an accent by playing a note louder or by giving its attack more emphasis.
- Some accents come from the music itself rather than from performance.
- A long note can sound accented because its duration gives it extra weight.
- An unusually high or low note can sound accented because it stands out from the surrounding register.
- A dissonant or chromatic note can sound accented because its tension makes it more noticeable.
- Accent can therefore come from loudness, attack, duration, register, or harmonic tension.

# meter and metrical accent
- *Meter* is a repeating pattern of strong and weak beats.
- A *strong beat* receives more emphasis; a *weak beat* receives less.
- The position of the strongest beat determines the basic meter.
- *Duple meter* groups beats in twos: strong–weak.
- *Triple meter* groups beats in threes: strong–weak–weak.
- *Quadruple meter* groups beats in fours, with the strongest accent on beat 1 and a weaker secondary accent on beat 3.
- The strong accent on the first beat of a measure is called the *metrical accent*.
- Beat subdivisions also have their own accent pattern.
- The beginning of a beat is stronger than the subdivisions that follow it.
- In a division by two, the first subdivision is stronger than the second.
- In a division by three, the first subdivision is stronger than the next two.
- In slower music, larger subdivisions can develop secondary accents that mirror the meter of the measure.

# time signatures
- A *time signature* tells how beats are organized into measures.
- It contains two numbers.
- The top number usually tells how many beats are in each measure.
- The bottom number tells which note value represents the beat.
- Common simple meters use quarter-note beats:
- 2/4 = 2 beats per measure
- 3/4 = 3 beats per measure
- 4/4 = 4 beats per measure
- Other note values can represent the beat, such as the half note or eighth note.
- Some meters group beats into larger units.
- *Compound meter* groups beats into threes.
- Common compound meters include 6/8, 9/8, and 12/8.
- In 6/8, the six eighth notes are usually grouped as 3 + 3.
- At a slow tempo, those six eighth notes may be felt individually.
- At a faster tempo, 6/8 is usually felt as two large beats, each divided into three parts.
- Some meters use irregular groupings such as 5 or 7 beats.
- These often combine groups of 2 and 3.
- Meter can change during a piece, producing a new time signature.

# rhythmic accent versus metrical accent
- A *metrical accent* comes from the meter itself: some beats are naturally stronger than others.
- A *rhythmic accent* comes from the music, such as a long note, loud note, or emphasized attack.
- Rhythm feels straightforward when rhythmic accents reinforce the strong beats of the meter.
- *Syncopation* occurs when a rhythmic accent falls on a metrically weak beat or weak part of a beat.
- One common syncopation begins a note on a weak beat and holds it across the following strong beat.
- This makes the note’s attack emphasize the weak position while the expected strong beat receives no new attack.
- Repeated syncopations can create a temporary accent pattern that conflicts with the written meter.
- In triple or compound meter, accents can regroup the same notes from groups of three into groups of two.
- This temporary regrouping is called *hemiola*.
- Hemiola changes the way the meter is felt without necessarily changing the written time signature.
- Normal alignment: metrical accent + rhythmic accent on the same strong beat
- Syncopation: rhythmic accent on a weak beat or subdivision
- Hemiola: 3 + 3 felt temporarily as 2 + 2 + 2

# rhythmic groups
- A *rhythmic group* is a small pattern of notes heard as one unit.
- Rhythmic groups help organize the flow of a melody or passage.
- A rhythmic group can begin on a strong beat.
- It can also begin on a weak beat or subdivision and continue into a stronger beat.
- Rhythmic groups do not have to match measure boundaries.
- A group can extend across a bar line or last for more than one measure.
- One rhythmic group can connect directly into the next.
- When the ending of one group also functions as the beginning of the next, the groups *overlap*.
- Rhythmic grouping therefore describes how notes are heard as phrases or units, not simply how they are divided by bar lines.

# measure groups and phrases
- Meter can organize not only beats within a measure, but also whole measures into larger groups.
- In these larger groups, some measures feel stronger and others weaker.
- A *hypermeasure* is a group of measures that behaves like one large measure.
- The broader pattern of strong and weak measures is called *hypermeter*.
- Four-bar hypermeasures are common and often feel like strong–weak–strong–weak.
- Two smaller measure groups can combine into one larger four-bar group.
- Three-bar hypermeasures can also occur.
- A *phrase* is a larger musical unit whose rhythmic grouping also leads toward a tonal goal.
- A phrase often ends when the harmony or melody reaches a point of arrival.
- Four-bar and eight-bar phrases are common.
- Phrases do not have to be symmetrical; five-bar, seven-bar, and other lengths also occur.
- Beat → measure → hypermeasure → phrase

# rhythm and dissonance treatment: dissonance, duration, accent
- A *dissonance* gets its effect from how it relates to the consonances around it.
- Dissonances often arise from stepwise melodic motion.
- They also tend to occur on shorter notes and in metrically weak positions.
- This makes the dissonance sound temporary and connected to a more stable goal.
- *Passing tones* and *neighboring tones* therefore usually occur on weak beats or weak parts of beats.
- When a passing or neighboring tone falls on a strong metric position, it becomes an *accented passing tone* or *accented neighboring tone*.
- An *appoggiatura* is an accented dissonant tone that typically arrives on a strong beat and then resolves by step.
- Accenting or lengthening a dissonance increases its prominence and tension.
- Weak-position dissonance = less emphasized
- Strong-position dissonance = accented dissonance

# rhythm and dissonance treatment: suspensions
- A *suspension* is a dissonance created when a note is delayed into the next harmony.
- The note begins as part of the earlier harmony and continues into a strong beat where the surrounding harmony has changed.
- Because the old note is still sounding against the new harmony, it becomes dissonant.
- The suspended note then moves to the note that normally belongs there, restoring consonance.
- Suspensions therefore create the pattern: consonance → accented dissonance → consonance.
- A suspension often comes from *syncopation*: a note begins before a strong beat and is held across it.
- The suspended note can be tied across the beat or struck again on the strong beat.
- In music with several parts, a suspension can delay the arrival of one note of a chord while the other chord tones have already arrived.
- Suspension = delay a note → create tension on a strong beat → resolve to the expected note

# rhythm and dissonance treatment: anticipations
- An *anticipation* is a note from the next harmony that arrives early.
- It begins before the strong beat where that note properly belongs.
- Because the surrounding harmony has not changed yet, the early note may sound dissonant.
- When the next harmony arrives, the anticipated note becomes consonant.
- Anticipations therefore create the pattern: early arrival → temporary dissonance → expected harmony.
- The anticipated note may be held into the new harmony or played again when the new harmony arrives.
- In textures with several parts, the anticipated note often appears in the highest voice.
- Anticipation = next chord tone arrives early
